Pottery is often associated with the potter's wheel, but some of the most beautiful, organic ceramic pieces are made using only hands and a few simple wooden tools. This is known as handbuilding.

Handbuilding allows you to slow down, feel the clay, and create shapes that wheel-throwing can't replicate. In this starting guide, we will explore three basic techniques: pinch pots, coil pots, and slab building.

## 1. Safety and Setup for Home Pottery

If you are working with clay at home, setup is simple, but cleanliness is critical:

*   **Work Surface:** Work on a canvas sheet, a wooden board, or the back of a canvas painting. Clay will stick to smooth plastic tables.
*   **Clay Dust Warning:** Never let dry clay scrapings pile up. Wipe your table down with a damp sponge. Clay dust contains silica, which should not be inhaled.
*   **Choosing Clay:** For beginners, choose air-dry clay (if you don't have access to a local kiln) or a low-fire stoneware clay if you plan to fire and glaze your pieces later.

## 2. The Pinch Pot Technique

The pinch pot is the most natural way to start. It forms the base of many complex sculptures.

1.  Roll a piece of clay into a smooth ball about the size of a lemon.
2.  Hold the ball in your palm. Press your thumb down into the center of the ball, leaving about half an inch of clay at the bottom.
3.  Gently squeeze the walls of the pot between your thumb and fingers, rotating the ball in your hand. Keep your pinches gentle and uniform to create walls of even thickness.
4.  Smooth the rim with a damp finger. You now have a simple, rustic bowl.

## 3. Building with Coils

Coil building is an ancient technique used to create large pots, vases, and organic sculptures.

Roll out long, snake-like ropes of clay of uniform thickness (about the size of a marker). Coil the ropes on top of a flat clay base to build up the walls.

<blockquote>
  "In handbuilt pottery, the goal is not machine-like symmetry. Embrace the gentle wobbles and finger marks; they are the fingerprint of the artist."
</blockquote>

To join coils together so they don't split during firing, you must **score and slip**: scratch the surfaces that will touch using a needle tool, apply water or liquid clay (slip), and then press the coils together, smoothing the seams with your thumb or a wooden rib tool.

## 4. Slab Building: Creating Clean Planes

Slab building is perfect for geometric shapes like mugs, boxes, and rectangular flower vases.

Use a rolling pin to roll out flat sheets of clay of even thickness. Let the slabs dry slightly until they are "leather-hard" (stiff but still editable, like cheese). Cut out your panels, score and slip the joins, and reinforce the inner corners with a tiny clay coil to create solid, strong joints.
