A junk journal starts from the opposite premise of almost every other art supply on this site — instead of buying fresh materials, you're raiding the recycling bin, the junk drawer, and the bottom of an old shoebox. Torn envelopes, expired boarding passes, the blank backs of old greeting cards, a coffee-stained receipt that's oddly beautiful once you look at it as texture rather than trash. Junk journaling turns all of it into raw material, and the finished pages carry a genuinely lived-in, layered quality that's hard to fake with store-bought supplies alone.

This list covers 21 junk journal ideas organized around the real building blocks of a finished journal — covers, page spreads, functional pockets, embellishments, and the aging techniques that tie everything together. Nearly everything on this list can be made from scraps you probably already have within arm's reach.

## What You'll Need

The entire appeal of junk journaling is that the materials cost close to nothing — the skill is in noticing what's usable, not in buying the right supplies.

**Essentials:**
- A stack of scrap paper: old book pages, magazine pages, envelopes, cardstock packaging, brown paper bags
- A basic binding method — even a simple long-stitch binding with a needle and waxed thread, or a few large binder rings through punched holes
- A glue stick or craft glue, plus double-sided tape for anything too delicate for wet glue
- Scissors and a ruler for trimming pages to a consistent size

**Optional but useful:**
- A tea bag or used coffee grounds for aging plain paper
- A small paper punch or two, for adding consistent decorative holes or edges
- A collection of ephemera set aside specifically for journaling — old stamps, ticket stubs, ribbon scraps, pressed flowers
- A bone folder or the back of a spoon, for crisp folds when working with stiffer scrap materials like cardstock or cereal boxes

## The Two Skills That Hold a Junk Journal Together

Junk journaling looks purely decorative, but two specific skills are doing most of the actual work underneath the layering.

**Aging paper on purpose.** A plain sheet of printer paper reads as obviously modern next to genuine old scraps, which breaks the cohesive, lived-in feel most junk journals are going for. Steeping a used tea bag directly on damp paper, or brushing on cooled tea or coffee with a wide brush, tints paper toward a warm, aged tone in minutes. Crumpling the paper gently before it dries adds convincing texture, and a light pass with a heat tool (on a safe setting, kept moving) speeds up drying and deepens the tint slightly.

**Layering with intention, not just accumulation.** It's tempting to glue down everything at once, but the most satisfying junk journal pages usually have a clear focal element with two or three supporting layers around it, rather than a dozen competing scraps piled on top of each other. Build a page in stages: a background layer first (a full page of aged paper or fabric), a mid-layer of one or two larger elements (a photo, a pressed flower, a larger cutout), and a final layer of small accent details (stamps, washi tape, tiny cutouts) added last and sparingly.

## A Simple Binding That Actually Holds

Before diving into the ideas below, it's worth settling on a binding method, since it determines how your pages need to be prepared. The simplest reliable option for a beginner is a long-stitch binding: fold your gathered pages into small groups called signatures, punch a few evenly spaced holes along the fold of each, and sew through all the signatures at once with a needle and sturdy waxed thread, wrapping around the spine between each signature. It looks more advanced than it is, and unlike glue-only bindings, a stitched spine holds up even when pages are stuffed with thicker embellishments like fabric scraps or small pockets.

## Cover Ideas

The cover sets the tone for everything inside, and it's a good place to use your most interesting scrap material.

**1. A Fabric-Wrapped Cardboard Cover**
Wrap a scrap of worn fabric or an old shirt around stiff cardboard, securing the edges on the inside — an instantly tactile, textured starting point.

**2. A Collaged Map or Sheet Music Cover**
Cover a plain board in layered fragments of an old map or sheet music, sealed with a thin coat of diluted glue for durability.

![Junk journal cover collaged from layered fragments of an old map and sheet music](/images/junk-journal-ideas-21-ways-turn-scraps-into-art/junk-journal-map-collage-cover-idea.webp)

**3. A Book Cover Repurposed as a Journal Cover**
An old, discarded hardcover book with a striking spine or embossed title makes an instant, ready-made cover — just need new pages bound inside.

**4. A Layered Envelope Pocket Cover**
Build the front cover from several overlapping vintage envelopes, creating built-in storage pockets right on the outside of the journal.

![Junk journal cover made from layered vintage envelope pockets, showing visible texture and aged paper tones](/images/junk-journal-ideas-21-ways-turn-scraps-into-art/junk-journal-envelope-cover-idea.webp)

## Page Spread Ideas

The interior pages are where the real layering and storytelling happens.

**5. A Pressed Botanical Spread**
Dried, pressed flowers and leaves arranged across a two-page spread, secured with small strips of tape rather than heavy glue to keep them from crumbling.

**6. A Vintage Recipe Card Spread**
Old handwritten or printed recipe cards, layered with matching kitchen ephemera like a torn grocery list or a flour-stained paper scrap.

**7. A Travel Ticket Collage Spread**
Boarding passes, train tickets, and museum stubs collaged together with a hand-drawn or written note about the memory attached.

![Junk journal page spread collaging travel tickets, boarding passes, and museum stubs with a handwritten note](/images/junk-journal-ideas-21-ways-turn-scraps-into-art/junk-journal-travel-ticket-spread-idea.webp)

**8. A Handwritten Letter Pocket Spread**
An old letter or postcard tucked into a paper pocket built directly into the page, inviting the reader to actually pull it out.

**9. A Torn-Edge Layered Color Spread**
A purely visual spread with no specific theme — just torn paper in a cohesive color family, layered for texture and rhythm alone.

![Junk journal page spread with pressed dried flowers and leaves secured with small strips of tape](/images/junk-journal-ideas-21-ways-turn-scraps-into-art/junk-journal-botanical-spread-idea.webp)

## Pocket and Fold Ideas

Functional elements that add interactivity and hidden storage throughout the journal.

**10. A Library Card Pocket**
A classic, instantly recognizable pocket style, glued directly onto a page and perfect for tucking in a small note or photo.

**11. A Fold-Out Accordion Page**
Several pages joined and folded accordion-style, unfolding into one long continuous spread — great for a timeline or an extended list.

**12. A Triangle Corner Pocket**
Fold a scrap of decorative paper into a simple triangle and glue two edges down, creating an instant corner pocket for a small photo or card.

**13. A Hidden Flap Page**
A smaller flap glued at one edge only, concealing a hidden note, quote, or small drawing underneath when lifted.

![Junk journal page featuring a vintage-style library card pocket glued onto layered paper](/images/junk-journal-ideas-21-ways-turn-scraps-into-art/junk-journal-library-pocket-idea.webp)

## Embellishment Ideas

Small final touches that finish a page without overwhelming it.

**14. Hand-Stamped Postage Details**
Real or reproduction vintage postage stamps, arranged in a small cluster to anchor a corner of a page.

**15. Washi Tape Borders**
Torn or cut strips of patterned tape used sparingly along a page edge or between two layered elements, rather than covering large areas.

**16. Twine and Button Accents**
A small loop of twine wrapped around a button and stitched or glued onto a page corner, adding physical texture beyond flat paper.

**17. A Wax Seal Stamp Detail**
A small pressed wax seal (real or a paper approximation) adds an instantly old-world, tactile focal point to an otherwise flat page.

![Junk journal page corner detail featuring vintage postage stamps and a small wax seal](/images/junk-journal-ideas-21-ways-turn-scraps-into-art/junk-journal-embellishment-detail.webp)

## Ephemera Sourcing and Aging Ideas

The final four ideas are less about a single page and more about building your ongoing supply.

**18. A "Scrap Bag" Habit**
Keep a single bag or box where anything visually interesting — an unusual receipt, a torn bit of packaging — goes automatically, rather than being thrown away, building a working stash without a dedicated shopping trip.

**19. Thrifted Book Pages**
Damaged secondhand books, too worn to sell or donate, are one of the best sources of aged, genuinely old paper — check thrift stores and library sale bins.

**20. A Batch Tea-Staining Session**
Age a dozen sheets of plain paper at once in a single session, so you always have a ready supply of aged base paper on hand for future pages.

**21. Family Ephemera With Permission**
Old family photos, letters, or documents (copies, not irreplaceable originals) can turn a junk journal into something genuinely personal and archival rather than purely decorative.

## Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

A handful of habits separate a junk journal that holds together from one that falls apart within a few weeks of use.

- **Using wet glue on delicate ephemera.** Dried flowers, thin vintage paper, and fabric scraps often warp or tear under wet glue. Reach for double-sided tape or small adhesive dots for anything fragile.
- **Overloading a single page.** More embellishment isn't automatically better — a page with one clear focal element and a few supporting layers reads as more intentional than one crammed with everything at once.
- **Skipping a real binding method.** Loose pages glued only at a spine tend to fall apart quickly once the journal is handled or stuffed with thicker items. A proper stitched or ringed binding is worth the extra ten minutes.
- **Ignoring page thickness variance.** A journal built entirely from thick cardstock and fabric scraps becomes bulky and hard to close. Mix in plenty of thin paper pages to keep the overall journal flexible.

![Split comparison showing an overloaded, poorly bound junk journal page falling apart on one side and a well-layered, properly bound version on the other](/images/junk-journal-ideas-21-ways-turn-scraps-into-art/junk-journal-common-mistakes-comparison.webp)

## Keeping Your Journal From Falling Apart

A junk journal gets handled more than most finished art pieces — flipped through, added to, carried around — so durability matters more here than in a piece meant to hang on a wall. Reinforce your spine with a strip of fabric tape or cloth book tape along the inside fold before binding, especially if you're using a lot of thicker mixed-media pages. If a page feels genuinely too fragile to survive regular handling (a very old, brittle piece of ephemera, for instance), consider photocopying or scanning and printing a copy to use in the journal instead, and keeping the fragile original stored separately and safely.

## Final Thoughts

Junk journaling rewards resourcefulness more than any craft supply budget — the best pages in this list often come from material that would otherwise have been thrown away. Start with a simple cover and a few pocket pages to get comfortable with your binding method, then build toward the denser botanical and ephemera spreads once layering with intention, not just accumulation, starts to feel natural.
