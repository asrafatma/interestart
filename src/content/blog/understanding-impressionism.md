In the late 19th century, a group of Parisian artists staged a rebellion that would change the trajectory of art history forever. Rejecting the dark, highly polished historical scenes approved by the official French Académie des Beaux-Arts, they stepped outside to paint fleeting moments of modern life.

This is the story of Impressionism—an art movement focused on light, color, and the beauty of the everyday.

## 1. The Rebellion Against the Salon

To understand the Impressionists, we must look at what they were fighting against. The official Paris Salon favored grand historical, mythological, and biblical subjects painted with invisible brushstrokes and dark, earthy glazes.

The rebels—Claude Monet, Camille Pissarro, Edgar Degas, and Pierre-Auguste Renoir—wanted to capture *modernity*. They painted train stations, crowded cafes, city streets, and sunlight filtering through trees. They wanted to capture how the eye perceives a scene in a single, passing second.

## 2. Key Techniques: The Impressionist Brushstroke

Impressionist paintings look distinctly different from classic academic works due to several technical shifts:

*   **En Plein Air (Outdoors):** The invention of pre-mixed paint in metal tubes allowed artists to carry their easels outside, capturing changing daylight directly.
*   **Broken Color:** Instead of mixing pigments thoroughly on a palette, they applied distinct strokes of pure color side by side on the canvas. From a distance, these colors blend optically in the viewer's eye.
*   **Wet-on-Wet Painting:** Applying wet paint over wet layers created soft, blended boundaries that evoke the haze of atmosphere.
*   **No Pure Black:** The Impressionists avoided black paint. They realized that shadows in nature are never truly black—they are filled with blue, purple, and green reflections.

## 3. Claude Monet: The Master of Light

Claude Monet was the driving force of the movement. His painting *Impression, Sunrise* (1872) gave the movement its name, originally coined as a criticism by a hostile journalist.

Monet was fascinated by how light transforms a single subject over time. He painted series of the same cathedral, haystacks, and water lilies at different hours of the day, proving that color is not an inherent property of an object, but rather a reflection of light.

<blockquote>
  "When you go out to paint, try to forget what objects you have before you—a tree, a house, a field... Merely think, here is a little square of blue, here an oblong of pink, here a streak of yellow, and paint it just as it looks to you." — Claude Monet
</blockquote>

## 4. Why Impressionism Matters Today

Impressionism paved the way for modern abstract art. By demonstrating that an artwork could focus on the *medium* itself (paint, texture, color) rather than serving as a photographic copy of reality, they liberated artists to paint subjective emotion and light.

Next time you look at an Impressionist painting, stand close to see the chaotic mosaic of thick, visible brushstrokes. Then step back and watch the strokes resolve into a luminous, atmospheric scene.
