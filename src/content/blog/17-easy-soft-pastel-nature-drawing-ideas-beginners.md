Soft pastels are one of the most forgiving mediums for drawing nature, and that's exactly why they're such a good place to start. The color goes down fast, blends with just a finger or a blending stump, and forgives the kind of small imperfections that would stress you out in a tighter medium like colored pencil. Nature subjects are especially kind to beginners too — a hillside, a cloud, or a single leaf doesn't need to be anatomically perfect to read as believable. It just needs the right values and a little softness where things should be soft.

This list covers 17 nature drawing ideas built specifically around what soft pastels do well: big atmospheric skies, soft color transitions, textured foliage, and small close-up studies you can finish in under an hour. Each one includes the specific technique it's meant to teach you, so you're not just filling a page — you're building real skills.

## What You'll Need for Soft Pastel Nature Drawing

Soft pastels are forgiving, but the wrong paper or tools can still make the experience frustrating. Here's what actually matters.

**Essentials:**
- A set of soft pastels (12–24 colors is plenty to start; you don't need artist-grade for your first pieces)
- Textured or sanded pastel paper (regular printer paper won't hold enough pigment)
- A kneaded eraser (it lifts pastel without smearing it, unlike a rubber eraser)
- A few blending stumps or tortillons
- Workable fixative spray

**Optional but useful:**
- Pastel pencils for fine details like grass blades or bird feathers
- A soft-bristle brush for lifting excess pigment
- Black or dark-toned pastel paper for night sky pieces
- A limited earth-tone palette (a few greens, browns, and warm neutrals) if you want to keep your color choices simple while you're learning

## The Two Techniques You'll Use in Almost Every Idea Below

Before jumping into the list, it helps to understand the two moves that make soft pastel nature drawings actually look soft.

**Blending.** Soft pastel blends by smudging pigment with your finger, a blending stump, or a soft cloth. For skies, water, and distant hills, blend in the direction the subject naturally moves — horizontally for a flat horizon, in soft circular motions for clouds. Blend the underlying color first, then add sharper details on top so they don't get smudged away.

**Layering.** Soft pastels build up in layers rather than one flat coat. Lay down a base color, blend it, then add a second, slightly different color on top without fully blending it in — this is what gives skies and foliage their depth instead of looking like one flat block of color. Save your sharpest, most saturated pastel marks for last, on top of the blended layers underneath.

## A Simple Color Approach for Nature Scenes

One of the fastest ways to make a beginner pastel drawing look more intentional is to limit your palette before you start. Nature scenes rarely need more than five or six colors working together — a warm and a cool green, one or two earth tones, a warm neutral for light, and a deeper shade for shadow. Pick your palette before you start blocking in shapes, and pull every color for that piece from it rather than reaching for whatever stick is closest. This single habit does more to make a drawing feel cohesive than almost any technical skill, and it removes a decision that trips up a lot of beginners: not knowing which green, brown, or blue to reach for next.

## Landscape Scenes

Wide, simple landscapes are the best starting point for soft pastels because they're mostly large blended shapes with very little fine detail required.

**1. Rolling Hills at Golden Hour**
Block in a warm gradient sky first, then layer soft green and gold tones across the hills, blending each hill slightly lighter than the one behind it to create depth. This teaches basic atmospheric perspective — distant shapes should always look a little softer and paler.

**2. Simple Meadow with Wildflowers**
Lay down a blended green base for the field, then use the tip of a pastel stick to dot in small flowers on top, unblended. Keeping the flowers crisp against a soft, blended background is what makes them pop.

**3. Distant Mountain Silhouette**
Practice value control here: the closest mountain range should be the darkest and most detailed, with each range behind it lighter and softer. This is one of the fastest ways to make a flat drawing feel like it has real distance.

**4. Countryside Path Through Trees**
A dirt path leading into a row of trees is a great composition exercise. Blend the path lighter in the center to suggest sunlight breaking through the tree line, and keep the tree shapes loose rather than individually detailed.

## Sky and Weather Studies

Skies are one of soft pastel's best subjects because the medium blends into smooth gradients almost effortlessly.

**5. Sunset Sky Study**
Layer warm oranges and pinks near the horizon, blending upward into cooler purples and blues near the top of the page. Blend the transition zones heavily, but leave a few unblended pastel strokes near the horizon for texture.

**6. Soft Cloudy Sky**
Practice negative-space drawing by blending your sky color first, then lifting pigment out with a kneaded eraser to "draw" the clouds in reverse. This is a genuinely useful pastel-specific trick that doesn't work the same way in most other mediums.

**7. Starry Night Sky**
Working on black or dark blue pastel paper, block in a soft blended glow near the horizon, then use a pastel pencil or the sharp corner of a pastel stick to add small, scattered white dots for stars. The dark paper does most of the contrast work for you.

![Soft pastel sky study showing a warm sunset gradient blending into a cooler blue upper sky](/images/17-easy-soft-pastel-nature-drawing-ideas-beginners/soft-pastel-sunset-sky-study.webp)

## Trees and Plants

Foliage is where layering really pays off — a single flat green will never read as convincingly as two or three layered tones.

**8. Single Tree Silhouette**
Draw one tree against a simple sky, focusing entirely on shape and silhouette rather than individual leaves. This is a good exercise in confident, decisive mark-making.

**9. Autumn Leaves Close-Up**
A cluster of fallen leaves is a perfect small study in warm color layering — reds, oranges, and browns blended together, with a few sharp, unblended edges to suggest the crisp texture of dry leaves.

**10. Pine Branch Study**
Practice directional mark-making by pulling short, quick pastel strokes outward from a central branch line, varying the pressure so some needles are darker and more defined than others.

**11. Wildflower Bouquet**
A loose bouquet of mixed wildflowers lets you practice color variety in a small, forgiving composition. Keep the vase or jar simple and let the flowers carry the color interest.

![Soft pastel study of a single tree silhouette against a simple blended sky](/images/17-easy-soft-pastel-nature-drawing-ideas-beginners/soft-pastel-tree-silhouette-study.webp)

## Water and Reflections

Water is one of the more advanced subjects on this list, but soft pastel actually makes reflections easier than most mediums because blending mimics the softness of light on water naturally.

**12. Calm Lake Reflection**
Draw the shoreline and trees first, then repeat the same shapes upside down beneath the waterline in slightly muted, more blended tones. The reflection should always be a little softer and less saturated than the real object above it.

**13. Ocean Waves at Dusk**
Focus on horizontal blending for the water and layered warm tones for the sky, with a few short, unblended white or pale-blue pastel strokes along the top of each wave to suggest foam catching the light.

**14. Rain-Soaked Window with a Blurred Nature View**
A more creative option: draw a soft, blurred landscape as if seen through a rainy window, heavily blended throughout, with a few vertical unblended lines to suggest raindrops running down the glass.

![Soft pastel drawing of a calm lake with a softly blended reflection of trees and shoreline](/images/17-easy-soft-pastel-nature-drawing-ideas-beginners/soft-pastel-lake-reflection.webp)

## Small Nature Details

Close-up studies are ideal when you want a finished piece without committing to a full scene, and they're some of the best subjects for practicing texture.

**15. Feather Study**
A single feather is a great exercise in directional strokes and subtle color shifts along the shaft. Keep the background simple or blended so the feather stays the clear focal point.

**16. Pinecone Close-Up**
Layer warm browns from dark to light, using the edge of the pastel stick to create the individual scale-like segments. This is a satisfying subject for practicing texture without needing fine detail work.

**17. Single Mushroom on Forest Floor**
A small mushroom against a blurred, blended forest-floor background is an easy, low-pressure way to end a practice session — simple shape, a little color variation, and a soft background that takes almost no time to finish.

## Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

A few small habits separate a muddy pastel drawing from a soft, believable one.

- **Overblending everything.** If every mark is blended, the drawing loses depth. Always save a few crisp, unblended strokes for the very last layer.
- **Skipping the paper's texture.** Smooth paper can't hold enough pastel layers. Sanded or textured paper is worth the extra cost while you're learning.
- **Going straight to detail.** Block in large shapes and values first. Detail added too early gets blended away or looks disconnected from the rest of the piece.
- **Using too many colors at once.** A limited palette of 5–6 related tones almost always looks more cohesive than reaching for every color in the set.

## Finishing and Protecting Your Drawing

Soft pastel smudges easily even after it's finished, so a few last steps matter.

Spray workable fixative in light, even coats from about 12 inches away, letting each layer dry before adding another. Fixative will slightly deepen your darks and can mute some of the brightness in your lightest layers, so it's worth doing a test spray on a scrap piece first if you're using a new brand. Once fully dry, store finished pieces with a sheet of glassine or wax paper between them, and frame under glass with a mat spacer so the glass never touches the pastel surface directly.

## Which Idea Should You Start With?

If you're brand new to soft pastels, start with the landscape scenes or sky studies rather than jumping straight to the close-up subjects. Large blended shapes are far more forgiving than small detailed ones, and skies in particular teach the blending and layering skills every other idea on this list relies on. Once a soft gradient sky feels comfortable, move on to a single tree silhouette or a lake reflection before attempting the small nature details — those close-up studies look effortless in a finished piece, but they actually demand more control over pressure and edge quality than the wider scenes do. Treat the first few ideas as practice for the ones that come later, rather than trying to make every piece a finished, frameable drawing.

## Final Thoughts

Soft pastel rewards exactly the kind of practice nature subjects are good for — big shapes, soft transitions, and small forgiving details you can finish in a single sitting. Start with the landscapes and sky studies if you're brand new to the medium, since they teach blending and layering without demanding much precision. Once those feel comfortable, the close-up studies are where soft pastel really starts to shine.
