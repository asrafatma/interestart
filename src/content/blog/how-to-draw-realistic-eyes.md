Drawing a realistic eye is one of the most rewarding milestones for a sketching student. While it looks incredibly complex, the eye can be broken down into basic geometric forms, light sources, and pencil value ranges.

In this guide, we will step away from stylized drawing and look closely at anatomical structure, highlights, and contrast.

## 1. Understanding Eye Anatomy and Structure

Before putting pencil to paper, we must understand the shape of the eye. A common mistake is drawing the eye as a flat football shape. In reality, the eyeball is a sphere nestled inside a socket, wrapped by the eyelids.

*   **The Sclera (White of the Eye):** It is a curved surface. This means it must be shaded at the corners to show spherical volume.
*   **The Tear Duct (Caruncle):** A small fleshy structure at the inner corner that catches subtle highlights.
*   **The Iris and Pupil:** The iris is a muscle fibers ring, and the pupil is an opening (a black hole). The iris is partially covered by the upper eyelid.

## 2. Laying Down the Structural Outline

Start with a light 2B pencil. Avoid applying heavy pressure, as you will need to erase guidelines later.

1.  Sketch a circle representing the eyeball.
2.  Draw a faint horizontal line through the center to guide alignment.
3.  Draw the upper and lower eyelids wrapping over the sphere. Notice that the highest point of the upper eyelid is usually closer to the tear duct, while the lowest point of the lower eyelid is closer to the outer edge.
4.  Map the circle of the iris. Ensure it overlaps with the upper lid slightly so the eye doesn't look startled.

## 3. Creating Value Contrast and Shading

For realistic shading, we need a range of graphite pencils (specifically 2H for light details, 2B for base tones, and 6B for deep blacks).

First, darken the pupil using your darkest 6B pencil. Leave a crisp, white box or circle touching the pupil to represent the primary highlight. This white space must remain untouched by pencil lead.

Next, shade the iris. Draw lines radiating outward from the pupil to the edge of the iris, like spokes on a bicycle wheel. Leave some areas lighter to represent highlights reflecting off the wet surface.

<blockquote>
  "The secret to realism is value contrast. Don't be afraid to make the darks dark enough to make the highlight sparkle."
</blockquote>

## 4. Drawing Eyelashes and Final Details

Eyelashes do not grow in straight, parallel lines. They grow in clusters that curve outwards and overlap.

Use a sharp 4B pencil. Flick the pencil from the root of the lash outward, releasing pressure at the end of the stroke to create a tapered tip. The eyelashes in the center of the eye point upward, while those on the sides fan out toward the temples.

Finally, draw the crease of the upper eyelid. Add subtle shadows beneath the upper lashes hitting the eyeball. This cast shadow immediately anchors the eyelids onto the sphere, giving the drawing photographic depth.
