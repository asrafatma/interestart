For roughly six hundred years, one color in a painter's studio was priced the same way gold was: by weight, against gold's own market rate, sometimes exceeding it. That color was ultramarine, and the reason had nothing to do with fashion or rarity of taste. It came down to geography, chemistry, and an almost absurd amount of manual labor — a single blue stone that had to travel thousands of miles, get pulverized, kneaded, and washed for days, before a painter could touch a brush to it.

This is the story of that blue: where it came from, why it cost what it cost, who it bankrupted, and how two very different reinventions — one in a 19th-century French chemistry contest, one in a 20th-century Parisian art studio — changed what the color could mean.

## Where Ultramarine Actually Comes From

Ultramarine isn't a synthetic invention or a mixed hue. In its original form, it's ground lapis lazuli — a semi-precious metamorphic rock flecked with gold-colored pyrite and white calcite running through a deep, almost electric blue. For most of history, there was essentially one source worth mining: the Sar-e-Sang mines in the Badakhshan province of northeastern Afghanistan, tucked into the Hindu Kush mountains. Lapis had been extracted there since at least the third millennium BCE, but it took until the medieval period for the stone to be reliably processed into a stable, workable paint pigment.

The name itself records the journey. "Ultramarine" comes from the medieval Latin *ultramarinus* — literally "beyond the sea." To a European buyer in Venice or Florence, that's exactly what it was: a pigment that had crossed an ocean's worth of overland and maritime trade routes just to reach the harbor, before a single ounce reached an artist's studio.

![Illustrated map tracing the historical overland and maritime trade route carrying lapis lazuli stone from the Badakhshan mines to Venetian ports](/images/the-history-of-ultramarine-pigment-more-precious-than-gold/ultramarine-trade-route-map.webp)

Venice, as the dominant Mediterranean trading port of the era, became the pigment's main European gateway — which is also why so many of the most ultramarine-saturated paintings of the period come out of Venetian and northern Italian workshops rather than further inland, where the pigment had to pass through yet another round of merchants and markups before it arrived.

## The Labor Behind the Color

Here's the part most people skip past: lapis lazuli, straight out of the ground, does not make good paint. Crush the raw stone and you get a dull, greyish-blue powder, muddied by all the calcite and pyrite embedded in it. Getting to the pure, saturated blue pigment required a separation process that could take days and consumed most of the original stone as waste.

The method, refined and documented through the medieval and Renaissance periods, worked roughly like this: the stone was ground to a fine powder, then kneaded into a stiff dough with melted resin, wax, and oil. That dough was wrapped in cloth and submerged in a weak lye solution, then repeatedly kneaded and squeezed underwater. Each kneading released a small amount of pure blue pigment into the water, while the heavier grey and greenish impurities stayed trapped in the resin dough. The process was repeated — sometimes a dozen or more times — with each successive batch yielding a slightly lower grade of blue, from the finest, most saturated pigment in the first extractions down to a pale, ashy grey-blue in the last.

That grading is why period price lists don't quote a single figure for "ultramarine." They quote a range, sorted by extraction batch, with the first and purest grade costing dramatically more than the last. A workshop might use the cheapest grades for underpainting or shadow, reserving the finest, most expensive grade for a single, deliberate passage of color where it would actually be seen.

## A Color Reserved for the Sacred

By the time ultramarine reached Europe's painting workshops, its cost had already put it in a different category from every other pigment on the palette. A patron didn't just commission a painting — they often specified, in the contract itself, how much ultramarine could be used and where. It wasn't treated as a color choice. It was treated as a line item.

This is why, across centuries of religious painting, one detail repeats with startling consistency: the Virgin Mary's robe is blue. Not because blue was symbolically neutral or simply attractive, but because a patron paying for the most expensive pigment available wanted it visibly, unmistakably spent on the painting's most important figure. Ultramarine became a form of conspicuous devotion — proof, woven into the paint layer itself, that no expense had been spared for the sacred subject.

The painter and writer Cennino Cennini captured this reverence directly in his 1437 treatise *Il Libro dell'Arte* ("The Craftsman's Handbook"), devoting an entire chapter to ultramarine's preparation and calling it, in his own words, a color that is "noble, beautiful, and more perfect than all other colors." Cennini had studied under a pupil of Giotto, whose early-14th-century frescoes in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua remain one of the most famous surviving showcases of period ultramarine — an entire barrel-vaulted ceiling painted in a blue so extensive and so richly saturated that it was, by itself, a declaration of the chapel's importance.

## The Painters It Nearly Bankrupted

Ultramarine's cost didn't just shape what got painted blue — it shaped which paintings got finished at all, and which painters went into debt to use it.

Michelangelo's *The Entombment*, now in London's National Gallery, is unfinished in one very specific, very telling way: the figure intended to be the Virgin Mary, kneeling at the right edge of the composition, was left as bare underpainting. The most widely accepted explanation among art historians is that Michelangelo simply didn't have the ultramarine on hand to finish her robe, and the painting was abandoned before he could secure more.

Johannes Vermeer, working in Delft in the mid-17th century, used ultramarine far more liberally than most of his contemporaries — not just for skies and drapery, but mixed into shadows and underpainting where cheaper blues would have done the job invisibly to the viewer's eye. It was a habit later generations of art historians would specifically credit for the luminous, cool-toned quality of his interiors. It was also, by most accounts, a habit that contributed to the significant debts his family carried, debts severe enough that his widow petitioned to have some of them forgiven after his death, citing the ruinous cost of his materials.

These weren't isolated incidents. They were the predictable outcome of an entire pigment economy built around genuine physical scarcity — a scarcity that would remain completely unchanged for another three hundred years.

## The Contest That Ended the Scarcity

In 1824, the Société d'Encouragement pour l'Industrie Nationale in France announced a public prize: 6,000 francs to whoever could develop a viable method for producing synthetic ultramarine, cheaply and at scale. This wasn't an art-world initiative — it was an industrial one, aimed at everything from textile dyeing to porcelain glazing, where genuine ultramarine had always been too costly to use freely.

In 1826, French chemist Jean-Baptiste Guimet claimed the prize, having developed a process to synthesize the pigment from kaolin, sodium carbonate, sulfur, and other more accessible materials, fired at high temperature. The resulting compound was chemically near-identical to natural ultramarine — the same core sodalite structure responsible for the color — but it could be manufactured in a factory rather than mined and hand-processed from a single valley in Afghanistan.

The price collapse that followed was not gradual. Natural ultramarine in period Paris had been selling for somewhere in the range of 3,000 to 5,000 francs per pound for the finest grades. Guimet's synthetic version came to market at roughly 400 francs per pound — and continued to drop as production scaled. Within a generation, a color that had once been rationed by contract clause was available to any painter who could afford ordinary paint.

## What Actually Changed Once It Was Affordable

It's worth sitting with how strange this transition was for painters who had trained under the old economy. For six centuries, ultramarine had been a color you planned around — used sparingly, deliberately, in exactly the passage of a painting where its cost would register as meaning. Overnight, in historical terms, it became a color you could simply use.

Some of the shift shows up quietly: skies painted with more freedom and less caution, blues appearing in passages of shadow and reflected light where an earlier painter would have reached for a cheaper substitute like azurite or indigo instead. Some of it shows up more dramatically, later in the 19th century, as synthetic ultramarine became one of the standard blues in the newly mass-produced paint tubes that made outdoor, spontaneous painting possible for the Impressionists in the first place.

![Outdoor Impressionist-style painting scene showing an artist painting a sky freely with saturated ultramarine blue directly from a paint tube](/images/the-history-of-ultramarine-pigment-more-precious-than-gold/ultramarine-impressionist-skies.webp)

A color that had once been a patron's flex became, within a few decades, just another tube on the shelf. That didn't erase the pigment's mystique, though. It simply set the stage for a very different kind of reinvention, a century later.

## Yves Klein's Reinvention of a Very Old Blue

By the mid-20th century, ultramarine was an ordinary, affordable studio color — until the French artist Yves Klein decided it wasn't ordinary enough, and set out to restore some of what he felt got lost in the transition to mass production.

Klein's issue wasn't with the pigment itself but with how it was typically bound into paint. Standard oil or acrylic binders visibly dull raw pigment — anyone who has mixed dry pigment into a medium has seen the color shift and flatten slightly the moment the binder is added. Klein wanted the pigment's full, raw intensity preserved on the canvas, exactly as it looked as loose powder. Working with the Parisian paint supplier Édouard Adam, he developed a fixative using a synthetic resin, sold under the name Rhodopas, suspended in an appropriate solvent — a binder that held the ultramarine pigment in place without smothering its saturation the way conventional mediums did.

Klein first exhibited works using this technique in his 1957 show *Proposte Monocrome, Epoca Blu* ("Monochrome Proposals, Blue Epoch") in Milan — a room of canvases, all identical in size, all painted in nothing but this one intensely saturated blue. On May 19, 1960, he formalized the specific formulation with France's national patent office, registering it under the name International Klein Blue, or IKB.

Klein used IKB across an unusually wide range of work for the rest of his short career — not just flat canvases, but sponge reliefs, sculptures, and his well-known *Anthropométries*, in which he directed nude models covered in IKB paint to press and drag their bodies across canvas as a kind of human paintbrush. He described blue, in his own writing, in explicitly spiritual and immaterial terms — a color without dimension, without a fixed edge, standing in for something closer to open sky or open sea than to any bounded object. Klein died in 1962 at just 34, but IKB remains one of the most widely recognized single-pigment inventions in modern art, a direct descendant — chemically and symbolically — of the same blue mineral once measured out against gold.

## Using Ultramarine in Your Own Work Today

None of this history is only trivia — it's genuinely useful to know if ultramarine sits in your own paint box. Modern synthetic ultramarine (labeled as such, distinct from the pricier natural/genuine ultramarine some brands still sell in small quantities) is a warm-leaning blue with a slight violet undertone, which is worth knowing before you reach for it expecting a neutral or cool blue.

A few practical notes worth keeping in mind: ultramarine granulates noticeably in watercolor, settling into the texture of rougher paper in a way smoother blues like phthalo don't — many painters use this deliberately for skies and water rather than treating it as a flaw. It's also a relatively low-tinting-strength blue compared to phthalo or Prussian blue, meaning it takes more pigment to shift a mixture, which actually makes it more forgiving and controllable when mixing greys and violets. And because of its long, symbolically loaded history in devotional painting, it remains a genuinely good choice any time a piece calls for a blue that reads as weighty or reverent rather than bright or clinical.

If you want to feel a small echo of the original scarcity, genuine natural ultramarine — ground from real lapis lazuli — is still sold today by specialty pigment suppliers, at a price that, adjusted for what it actually is, still sits closer to a precious material than an art supply.

## Final Thoughts

Ultramarine's whole history runs on the same tension: a color valuable enough to be rationed, contracted for, and fought over, followed by a color made suddenly, permanently ordinary — and then, in Klein's hands, made to feel rare and singular all over again, not through scarcity this time but through sheer conviction about what one color could mean. Klein himself kept a personal trilogy of three colors he considered spiritually significant — blue, gold, and a specific saturated pink — an echo, probably unintentional, of exactly the pairing that made ultramarine so symbolically loaded in the first place: a blue once priced against gold, still capable of carrying that same weight centuries later.
